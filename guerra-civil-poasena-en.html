⚔️ Episodes of the Canton · 1925 – 1931

The Civil War of
Poás

The fascinating and tragic story of the fratricidal war that plunged the Canton of Poás into years of violence, fear, exile, and misfortune — a dark chapter that a veil of legend has never fully lifted.

📅 1925 · Conflict begins
⚰️ 1928 · Murder of Alberto "Chele"
🕊️ 1931 · Tragic end
↓ Read the story
"Why did our community find itself plunged into a spiral of fratricidal violence — practically a civil war — between 1925 and 1931?"

There was a convergence of many factors that might explain what happened: political and religious differences, hunger for power, caudillism, failed business dealings, intrigue, insults and slander, defense of honor, crimes and killings, ignorance, and recklessness.

What follows is an account based on the testimonies of don Eliécer Murillo Esquivel — who was very guarded and tends to attribute events to alcoholism and rage — complemented by the interpretation of historian Percy Rodríguez Argüello and some additional details. The true reasons remain far from clear, and a veil of legend still shrouds this era.

A resident of El Alto and member of the "mama Chica" clan — named after their mother Francisca Arrieta. His failed business deal with Alberto "Chele" sparked open warfare.

A Spanish priest who used the pulpit to attack his political opponents. His tense relationship with Alberto "Chele" was the first link in the chain of conflict.

Brother of Hernán Rodríguez. He shot Alberto "Chele" in cold blood at the Alajuela fair in 1928. Hunted for years, his final fate was tragic — he died by his own hand in 1931.

Witness and Chronicler
A narrator of events in later decades. A direct eyewitness of the era, he was guarded in his accounts and tended to explain events through the lens of alcoholism and bursts of rage.

Chapter I

The Seeds of Conflict

At the start of the 1920s, Alberto "Chele" Murillo owned the canton's hydroelectric plant and lived in "El Bajo." His estate was a genuine feudal domain, and his workers were completely loyal to him. But political clashes with the Spanish priest Venancio Oña — who used his pulpit to attack political adversaries — drove a wedge between Alberto "Chele" and the residents of "El Alto."

On one occasion, Father Oña slaughtered his beloved goat to host a feast for Alberto Echandi — a presidential candidate in 1923 — and the episode was mocked in newspaper caricatures of the time. Father Oña was no friend of Alberto "Chele" or his circle, which included the municipal attorney for Poás.

"While said attorney was visiting Poás, someone stole his horse and locked it inside Father Oña's property. The next morning, an animal was found sheared — but not the attorney's horse. It was Father Oña's."
— Account of don Eliécer Murillo Esquivel

The priest cursed: "The guilty hand behind this outrage shall fall." In the days that followed, a man named Chepe Chaves — a former laborer of Alberto "Chele" — had his hand crushed in a sugar mill accident. This incident fed the imagination and gossip of the townspeople.

The Shot on Election Day

On election day in 1923, amid liquor and excitement, Ricardo Conejo — nephew of Alberto "Chele" — fired shots at the parish house from horseback. Father Oña immediately and permanently abandoned Poás, after cursing the town.

Chapter II

Chronology of the Conflict

From broken promises to the final shot — six years that split the canton's history in two.

Background · 1923
The Shot at the Parish House
During the 1923 elections, Ricardo Conejo fires at the parish house from horseback. Father Venancio Oña abandons Poás for good, cursing the town. Tensions between "El Bajo" and "El Alto" deepen.
Crisis · 1926
The Failed Deal and Open War
Alberto "Chele" offers to buy Elicio Murillo Arrieta's farm, coveting a watercourse with a waterfall for a more powerful electric plant. They arrange to meet in Alajuela (1926), but Elicio never shows up. Alberto cannot forgive the snub. Days later, a bullet narrowly misses Alberto "Chele" in his rocking chair — he only survives by happening to rock forward. War is now openly declared.
1926 · Escalation
The Barbed Wire and the Shooting of Elicio
Alberto forbids Elicio from crossing his property, though Elicio ignores the ban since the shortcut saves him a long detour. The path is blocked with barbed wire; Elicio tears it down. Later he is shot in the abdomen and taken in critical condition to a clinic in San José, where he narrowly survives. Ricardo Conejo is suspected but never proven guilty. The Murillo Arrieta clan is enraged; Alberto "Chele" cuts off their electricity supply.
1926 · Terror
Curfew and Community Polarization
Tensions keep rising and the Alajuela command orders the Poás police force reinforced. Bar fights ending in gunshots over the slightest provocation become commonplace. A curfew is declared. The entire town is polarized. Tourism declines and many families are forced to migrate — the so-called Poás Diaspora — to Tilarán in Guanacaste, Cot in Oreamuno, and other locations.
May 22–23, 1926
The Death of Danilo Murillo
On the night of Saturday the 22nd through Sunday the 23rd of May 1926, both factions are drinking at a cantina. At ten o'clock the curfew sounds. An hour and a half later, the two groups meet armed at the southeast corner of San Pedro's plaza. In the confusion of the darkness, a bullet strikes Danilo Murillo — nephew of Alberto "Chele" — in the neck; he dies on the spot. Rumors point to Marcos Rodríguez or Lalo Murillo as the shooter. Hernán Rodríguez and Lalo flee Poás.
Dec. 30–31, 1927
The Ambush at El Tajo
Elicio Murillo is working as foreman of a road-building crew on the Poás–Alajuela highway. The group is ambushed on the road: gunfire rains down from the heights of "El Tajo." Elicio escapes, but Filadelfo "El Negro Pepo" Simpson and Lalo Murillo are fatally wounded. Rumor points to Lindor Salazar, a laborer of Alberto "Chele," as the perpetrator of the double killing.
January 31, 1928
The Death of Alberto "Chele" Murillo
On the day of the sugar-cane fair in Alajuela, Alberto "Chele" is standing on the east side of the market. A horseman shoots him dead in cold blood; one bystander is hit in the cheek. The horseman is Marcos Rodríguez, brother of Hernán. Hernán confesses to protect his younger brother, but it is later proven he could not have been there.
1928 – 1931 · Epilogue
The Flight and Fate of Marcos Rodríguez
Marcos Rodríguez flees, passing through La Maravilla and Santa Bárbara, where he abandons his horse. It is said he spent time outside the country. Over the years he returns to his village and keeps a low profile, until on the night of January 3, 1931 he is recognized at the wedding celebration of Lucía Solís and Elías Carballo. Arrested and taken to jail in Alajuela, he tells the political chief: "Pray to God that I never get out free."
September 3, 1931
The Final Act: The Death of Marcos Rodríguez
On the night of September 3, 1931, Marcos escapes from prison. A sentry at the prison gate stands in his way. According to Percy Rodríguez, Marcos takes his own life because he preferred to die rather than kill a friend. The darkest chapter in Poás history comes to a close.
Chapter III

Consequences & Impact

🏃
The Poás Diaspora
Many Poás families were forced to abandon the canton. The main destinations were Tilarán in Guanacaste and Cot in Oreamuno, among other towns.
📉
Economic and Tourism Decline
The curfew, insecurity, and damaged reputation caused a notable drop in tourism and commercial activity in the canton, especially in San Pedro.
⚡
End of the Electric Fiefdom
With the death of Alberto "Chele" Murillo, family control over the hydroelectric plant — the axis of economic and political power in the conflict — came to an end.
🌫️
A Veil of Legend
The true reasons and details of the conflict remain unclear. Generational silence and partial accounts built a story wrapped in mystery.

A Polarized Community

Poás was engulfed in a dark era of terror, chaos, and ill repute, particularly for San Pedro. The split of the community into two factions — "El Bajo" and "El Alto" — was not merely a family feud: it was a clash of political, economic, and personal agendas that drew in nearly the entire community.

Tensions ran so high that the Alajuela command had to reinforce the Poás police force. Bar brawls ending in gunfire over the slightest provocation became everyday occurrences. The curfew was a daily reality that terrified the population.

Final Reflection

Decades later, witnesses such as don Eliécer Murillo Esquivel — very guarded in his accounts — and historian Percy Rodríguez attempted to reconstruct the events. But memory is selective, and what remains of this story is a blend of verifiable facts, partial testimonies, and legends that time has intertwined.

What is clear is that Poás, like so many Costa Rican towns of that era, was not immune to humanity's most intense passions: honor, vengeance, power, and death. And from that darkness emerged, over the years, the prosperous, welcoming, and naturally beautiful canton we know today.
